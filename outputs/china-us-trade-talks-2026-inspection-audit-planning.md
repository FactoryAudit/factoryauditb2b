## Quick Answer

On **20 September 2026**, China and the United States concluded their **eighth round** of economic and trade consultations in New York. The Chinese Ministry of Commerce stated on **24 September** that the two sides reached consensus on issues including **reciprocal tariff reductions**, the **establishment of trade and investment councils**, and the **extension of the joint arrangement reached at the earlier Kuala Lumpur consultations**. The two sides also held their **first dialogue on artificial intelligence** under the consultation mechanism.

For a buyer, the practical consequence is narrower than the headlines suggest. **Firms do not stop making goods while negotiations continue, and an inspection does not become unnecessary because a tariff might fall.** What the talks change is *timing risk*: if a tariff change lands between production and shipment, the cost calculation on an order can move after the goods are already built. That is a reason to **inspect earlier and more granularly**, not to skip inspection.

---

## Definition

**Pre-shipment inspection** (PSI) is a shipment-specific check carried out before goods leave the factory: quantity, workmanship, specification and packing of a particular order are checked against the buyer's requirements, typically using an agreed sampling standard such as AQL. It says nothing about the factory's ongoing capability — that is a factory audit's question.

A **factory audit** is on-site and site-specific: it assesses whether the production facility can make your product and meets the standard you require.

The two services answer different questions, and trade-policy news changes the *timing pressure* on both, not the questions themselves.

## Why this matters for inspection, specifically

### The cost of a tariff change lands after production

A tariff is applied at the point of import, but the goods are built earlier. If a rate changes between the production date and the shipment date, the buyer carries the difference on goods that already exist. That asymmetry is why trade-policy uncertainty **increases** the value of inspecting early enough to act on the result.

### Early defects are cheaper than late defects

An inspection that finds a problem at 30% production lets you correct it while the line is still set up for your order. The same inspection at 95% production usually produces a rework-and-renegotiate conversation instead. In a period where the landed cost is uncertain, a defect you can still fix is worth more than a report you cannot act on.

### Inspection is not a substitute for a compliance review

Tariff arrangements and compliance requirements are different systems. A tariff reduction does not remove a forced-labour, chemical, or product-safety requirement. If your product is exposed to those regimes, the audit and document work still applies, and a clean inspection says nothing about them.

## Steps: planning inspection and audit work in an uncertain tariff window

### 1. Separate what is decided from what is assumed

Write two columns: measures **in force** today, and measures that are **agreed but not yet published**. Plan production and pricing on the first column. Do not let a headline about a future reduction change a purchase decision you are making now.

### 2. Fix the sequence, then scale it

For most orders the useful order is:

1. **Supplier verification** — is the company real and legally who it claims to be?
2. **Factory audit** — can this site make the product, to the standard you need?
3. **Pre-shipment inspection** — does *this batch* match the specification?

Scale each step to order value and product risk. Trade-policy uncertainty changes the *urgency* of step 3, not the order of the steps.

### 3. Move inspection earlier in the production cycle

For an order exposed to timing risk, add a **during-production check** at a defined milestone (commonly around 30% and 70%) rather than relying only on a final pre-shipment inspection. This converts a possible late finding into an early, correctable one.

| Checkpoint | Typical timing | What it can change |
|---|---|---|
| During-production (initial) | ~30% produced | Materials and workmanship; line still adjustable |
| During-production (mid) | ~70% produced | Pace, consistency, emerging defect trends |
| Pre-shipment inspection | 100% produced, packed | Ship/don't-ship decision; still correctable |
| Container loading | At loading | Quantity, packing, seal integrity |

### 4. Confirm the entity and the address — every time

A change in tariff treatment is a good moment to re-confirm the basics: which legal entity is exporting, and which site is producing. Buyers who discovered late that the exporting entity differed from the invoicing entity have found that tariff and customs treatment follows the entity, not the relationship.

### 5. Keep the audit scope on the standard, not on the tariff

If your customer mandates SMETA, BSCI or RBA, the audit scope is set by that requirement and is unaffected by trade talks. Do not let tariff news distract from a compliance deadline that has its own calendar.

### 6. Re-check before each new order, not once

Trade arrangements are revised through the consultation mechanism. A position that is correct in September may not be correct in December. Make the re-check a step in your order process, not a one-off task.

## Example: the same order, planned two ways

**Planned on the headline.** A buyer reads that reciprocal tariff reductions were agreed, assumes a lower landed cost, and builds that assumption into pricing. No early inspection is booked. At 90% production a workmanship issue surfaces; rework delays shipment past the window in which the assumed rate was available. Cost: rework plus a lost pricing assumption.

**Planned on the known position.** The same buyer plans on the rate in force, books a during-production check at 30%, catches a material substitution while the line is still flexible, and keeps the pre-shipment inspection as the final gate. When implementing documents are published, any reduction is upside. Cost: one additional inspection fee, against a corrected defect and a preserved pricing assumption.

The difference is not the news; it is whether the plan was built on measures in force or on measures still to be published.

## Checklist

- [ ] Measures in force separated from measures agreed but not yet published.
- [ ] Pricing built on the rate in force today, not on an announced future change.
- [ ] Supplier verification completed and the exporting entity confirmed.
- [ ] Production address confirmed as the site to be audited.
- [ ] Factory audit scoped to the required standard, if a standard is mandated.
- [ ] During-production check booked at a defined milestone for timing-exposed orders.
- [ ] Pre-shipment inspection retained as the final gate.
- [ ] Sampling standard (e.g. AQL) and defect definitions agreed in writing before inspection.
- [ ] Corrective-action path agreed in advance, including who decides on rework.
- [ ] Compliance requirements (forced labour, chemical, product safety) checked separately from tariff news.
- [ ] Re-check scheduled before the next order, not treated as a one-off.

## FAQ

**Q: Should I delay ordering until the tariff details are published?**
Delaying production has its own cost, and the timing of publication is not something a buyer controls. The more robust approach is to plan on the position in force, inspect early enough to stay flexible, and treat later changes as upside.

**Q: Does a tariff reduction mean I can skip inspection?**
No. Tariffs and product quality are unrelated. Inspection answers whether your goods match your specification; a tariff change does not alter that question.

**Q: Is a factory audit still needed if I already inspect every shipment?**
Yes, if your risk depends on the site's capability. Inspection is shipment-specific and cannot detect undisclosed subcontracting or a systemic process problem. The two cover different risks.

**Q: What does "reciprocal tariff reduction" actually mean for my product?**
As of 25 September 2026 the specific rates and product lists had not been published, so the answer depends on implementing documents that did not yet exist. Check the official publication before assuming any rate.

**Q: Where should I verify these developments?**
The Chinese Ministry of Commerce publishes its regular press conferences and notices directly. Use the primary source rather than a secondary summary of a headline.
