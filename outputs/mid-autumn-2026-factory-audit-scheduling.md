## Quick Answer

China's 2026 Mid-Autumn Festival runs **25–27 September** (Friday to Sunday, three days, no make-up workday). National Day runs **1–7 October** (seven days), with make-up workdays on **20 September** and **10 October**. For a factory audit, the practical consequence is not "the factory is shut on three days." It is that the **working days immediately around the holiday are the least representative days of the year to audit**: lines run partial shifts, supervisors are on leave, and records for the holiday period are incomplete. If you need a baseline that reflects normal operations, audit either **before 22 September** or **after 12 October**, and treat any visit inside 23 September–11 October as a scoped check rather than a full baseline.

## Definition

A **factory audit** is an on-site assessment of a production facility against a defined scope: the auditor visits the site, reviews records, observes processes, and interviews management and, where the scope includes social criteria, workers. The output is a report of findings at the time of the visit.

Because an audit records a **baseline on a specific day**, the day you choose is part of the method. A visit during a holiday-adjacent period produces a baseline of a factory that is not running normally — which is a different, and usually less useful, question than the one most buyers are asking.

## Why the holiday window matters more than the holiday itself

National holidays in China are set by the State Council. For 2026 the relevant arrangements are:

| Holiday | Dates | Make-up workdays |
|---|---|---|
| Mid-Autumn Festival | 25–27 September (Fri–Sun), 3 days | None |
| National Day | 1–7 October (Thu–Wed), 7 days | 20 September (Sun), 10 October (Sat) |

**Source:** General Office of the State Council, *Notice on Holiday Arrangements for 2026*; reported by CCTV (央视网) and CNR (央广网), 13 September 2026.

Three practical effects follow, none of which are visible if you only count the closed days:

1. **The week before the holiday is a push week.** Factories rush to ship before the break. Lines run at capacity, overtime rises, and quality checks are compressed. A record review in this window can look *stronger* than the factory's normal state.
2. **The first week after the holiday is a recovery week.** Staff return in waves, some lines restart late, and new workers are often onboarded to backfill turnover. A visit here can look *weaker* than the factory's normal state.
3. **The holiday period itself produces a records gap.** Payroll, attendance and production records for a holiday week are not representative. If the audit scope includes working hours or wage records, auditing a holiday week tells you little.

The point is not that audits during these weeks are impossible. It is that the **result has a different meaning**, and the report should say so.

## Steps

### 1. Decide what the audit is for, before you pick a date

- **Baseline audit** (you want to know the factory's normal state) → schedule outside the holiday-adjacent window.
- **Order-specific check** (you want to know whether *your* order is being made) → the calendar matters less; what matters is whether your production is actually running.
- **Compliance audit** (SMETA, BSCI, RBA or a customer-mandated standard) → confirm with the customer and the audit body whether a holiday-period visit is acceptable, because some programmes have rules about the period reviewed.

### 2. Work backwards from your shipment date

A factory audit is one step in a sequence, not a standalone event. For a Q4 shipment the commonly useful order is:

1. **Supplier verification** (documentary, remote, days) — confirms the company is real and legally who it claims to be.
2. **Factory audit** (on-site, scheduled) — confirms the site can make your product against the scope you set.
3. **Pre-shipment inspection** (shipment-specific) — confirms *this* batch matches your specification.

If the audit slips into the holiday window, the inspection date downstream is usually still achievable. Reordering the sequence to "inspect now, audit later" is usually the wrong trade, because inspection says nothing about the factory's ongoing capability.

### 3. Book the auditor before the factory goes quiet

Audit capacity tightens around Chinese national holidays for a simple reason: everyone who deferred a visit during the break wants to schedule in the same two weeks afterwards. Contact the audit provider **before** the holiday, not on the first working day back. State the production address, the scope, and whether the visit is announced or semi-announced.

### 4. Confirm the site will actually be producing

Before a holiday-adjacent visit, ask explicitly:

- Will your line for **my product** be running on that date?
- Will the production supervisor for that line be on site?
- Will records for the **previous full month** be available?

If the answer to any of these is no, the visit will produce observations about an empty or atypical factory. That is worth knowing in advance.

### 5. If the only available date is in the holiday window, change the scope

A scoped visit can still be valuable. Narrow it to what the day *can* answer — site existence, equipment present, warehouse and shipping activity, management interview — and state in the report that normal operations were not observed. A report that documents its own limitation is more useful than one that implies a baseline it did not capture.

## What a holiday-adjacent audit can and cannot tell you

| Question | Can a holiday-window audit answer it? | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Does the production site exist at the stated address? | Yes | Confirmed by physical presence on the day |
| Are the stated equipment and lines present? | Yes | Observed directly, even when idle |
| Is the factory operating at normal capacity? | No | Not observable during a holiday or push week |
| Are working hours and wage records consistent? | Limited | Holiday-period records are not representative |
| Is management present and responsive? | Partly | Depends on the week; supervisors are often on leave |
| Is my order physically in the factory? | Yes, if it is | Direct observation of your goods or line |

## Example: two buyers, same holiday, different outcomes

**Buyer A — baseline audit booked for 30 September.** The visit falls in the pre-holiday push and the early holiday window. Lines are running hard for other customers' orders; Buyer A's product is not scheduled that week. The report shows a busy factory, but nothing about whether Buyer A's product can be made to specification. Cost of the mistake: one audit fee and two weeks, plus a delayed decision.

**Buyer B — baseline audit booked for 15 October.** The factory has returned to normal operations, the previous full month's records are available, and the line for Buyer B's product is running. The report reflects a normal state and can be used as the baseline for follow-up. The trade-off is that Buyer B waited three weeks longer — which is why the decision should be made against the shipment date, not against the calendar.

## Checklist

- [ ] Audit purpose identified: baseline, order-specific, or compliance.
- [ ] Customer-mandated standard confirmed, including any rule on the period reviewed.
- [ ] Shipment date fixed, and the audit date worked backwards from it.
- [ ] Verification completed before the audit is booked.
- [ ] Auditor capacity secured *before* the holiday, not after.
- [ ] Production date for **your** product confirmed in writing.
- [ ] Line supervisor availability confirmed for the visit date.
- [ ] Previous full month's records confirmed as available.
- [ ] Announced or semi-announced approach decided.
- [ ] If the visit falls in the holiday window, scope narrowed and the limitation stated in the report.
- [ ] Follow-up and corrective-action expectations agreed in advance.

## FAQ

**Q: Is it pointless to audit during China's National Day holiday?**
No — but the result answers a narrower question. A visit inside the holiday window can confirm that the site exists, that stated equipment is present, and that your order is physically there. It cannot establish the factory's normal operating state. Say so in the report.

**Q: How far in advance should I book a factory audit around the holidays?**
Book before the holiday begins. The two weeks after a national holiday are the most congested period of the audit calendar, because deferred visits and new Q4 orders compete for the same slots.

**Q: Does the Mid-Autumn Festival affect shipping as much as National Day?**
Mid-Autumn is three days with no make-up workday, so its direct effect is smaller. Its indirect effect is larger than the day count suggests, because it sits immediately before the pre-National-Day shipping push.

**Q: Should I audit before or after placing the deposit?**
An audit answers whether the site can make the product; a deposit decision depends on that answer. For a first order of material value, auditing before releasing significant payment is the common approach. The holiday calendar does not change that logic — it only compresses the window in which you can act.

**Q: Do these dates apply to every factory?**
The public holiday dates are national. Whether a specific factory closes for all of them is a factory-level fact, not a national one. Confirm your supplier's own shutdown and restart dates directly; some factories run through part of the period and stagger leave.
